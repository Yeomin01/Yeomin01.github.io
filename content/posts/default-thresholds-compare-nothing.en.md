## Continuing

A short break from the load posts. Over the same period I was comparing our detection performance with other public guard models, and there too the way of measuring decided the conclusion.

I made a table: recall and false positive rate for each model, run at its default settings on public multi-turn conversation sets. One row stood out. **Our false positive rate 77%, the other models 0–1%.**

## A model that catches nothing has no false positives

Someone who saw the table asked: some of the other models have recall under 1%, so are they not simply catching nothing? They were right. The other models' recall was between 0.6% and 17%. At the default threshold they hardly flag anything. If you block nothing, false positives are 0.

My table was not comparing models. It was comparing **thresholds placed at different points**. One side sat at a point that catches a lot, the other at a point that catches almost nothing.

If a model outputs a score, you can move it to the same point and compare there. I produced three things.

- Separation (AUROC). How well it separates attack from benign, independent of the threshold.
- Recall with the false positive rate on benign data matched at 2%, 5%, and 10%.
- F1 at each model's best threshold. The threshold is picked on one half and measured on the other half.

## The conclusion changed in both directions

Matching the operating points did not change things only in our favor.

On a set of conversations generated by an attack tool, our separation was 0.957 and the other models were at 0.74–0.81. Recall at 5% false positives was 0.64 versus 0.32. This is the set that looked worst in the default-threshold table.

In the other direction, on hand-written intent-concealing attacks, separation was 0.96 and 0.88 for two other models and 0.77 for us. This is the set where we looked ahead in the default-threshold table (recall 0.55 versus 0.42). We only looked higher because we sat at a point that catches a lot. Our ability to separate was behind.

**In one table, the row that favored us and the row that went against us were both false.**

## The denominator of the 77% was wrong too

The second objection was about the number itself: why it came out as high as 77%, and whether it was a proper false positive rate.

The denominator was 900 "conversations generated by an attack tool given a harmless goal". When I read the raw text, three kinds were mixed together.

- In 300, the tool ignored the harmless goal and produced conversations that dig for information on harmful people and organizations. Only the label is benign. **Invalid.**
- 400 were harmless counterparts that share a topic with a harmful behavior. Even people would disagree on them. **Borderline.**
- 200 were everyday questions, such as how to roast a turkey. **Valid.**

Measured again on the 200 valid ones, it was 46.5%. Still high. And once there were more than five user turns, it rose to 90%. Reading the samples, the content was all harmless. They were flagged not because the topic was sensitive but because **the structure of the conversation was the attack tool's template**. This is a real weakness, and it had been buried under the 77%.

False positives on ordinary conversations also split by source. At five or more user turns, synthetic conversations in which a model also wrote the user side were at 60%, and real usage logs were at 5.6%. The latter is what can stand in for real traffic.

## A model where moving the threshold is not enough

One more thing got in the way. Our detector is not built as a threshold on a single score. There is a cumulative gate in the middle, and a conversation caught there goes out as an attack regardless of the threshold on the final score. Of the 103 false positives on ordinary benign conversations, 64 took that path.

So applying a different threshold to the saved scores does not give the operating points on the low false positive side. I have to change the sensitivity level and **run it again**. Even the assumption that one score file is enough to draw the curve needed checking first.

## Zooming out

A detection model's default threshold is a preference set by whoever built it. Some chose not to miss things, others chose not to get in the way. Read that choice as performance, and the model that catches nothing becomes the precise model.

The same goes for the control group. A "benign" label means trusting the process that produced it, but feeding a harmless goal into an attack tool did not produce harmless conversations. Reading the samples took less than an hour.

## Notes

- Compare detection models by recall at the same false positive rate and by separation. Do not put default-threshold numbers side by side.
- Check the control group by its raw text, not its labels. Split it into valid, borderline, and invalid, and use only the valid part as the denominator.
- First check whether the structure allows simply applying a different threshold to the scores. If there is a gate in the middle, the settings have to be changed and the model run again.

### References

- Fawcett, T. (2006). _An Introduction to ROC Analysis._ Pattern Recognition Letters. (how to compare classifiers independent of the threshold, and why a single-point figure is not enough)
- Davis, J., Goadrich, M. (2006). _The Relationship Between Precision-Recall and ROC Curves._ ICML. (cases where the two curves give different conclusions on problems where benign examples are overwhelmingly the majority)
- Jacob, D. et al. (2025). _PromptShield: Deployable Detection for Prompt Injection Attacks._ ACM CODASPY. (argues that detectors should be compared by detection rate at low false positive rates rather than by average metrics or default thresholds, and that the threshold should be set on a validation set)
